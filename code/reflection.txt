Completing this assignment taught me how much web scraping depends on matching
code to the structure of each HTML page. Lakeshore required class and ID-aware CSS
selectors, including a sibling selector and inner_text, while Hilltop worked better
with read_html and melt to reshape wide size columns. Canal Street required a
headless browser to select a shop, wait for JavaScript, paginate with "Show more",
crawl item links, and read attributes and item-page content. These techniques made
it clear that scraping is not just downloading HTML; it is controlling and observing
a page until the rendered data is available.

The hardest part was fuzzy matching. Normalization, token_sort_ratio, and the
AUTO_MATCH and REVIEW thresholds could find likely names, but scores could be
misleading. Matcha Latte looked close to Mocha Latte, while PSL needed an
owner-level decision. I learned to keep a human in the loop: the matcher proposes,
the owner decides, and record_decision preserves the answer for future runs. This
also showed me why owner decisions must win over automatic matches.

For gap analysis and price comparison, merge, indicator, pivot, and groupby made
it possible to compare structured data without row-by-row programming. I understand
how an outer merge separates "only us," "only them," and "both," and how min,
mean, max, and price_position classify market position. I want more practice reading
browser diagnostics and choosing selectors that remain tied to useful HTML structure.
My next step is to practice one small Playwright crawl and one pandas merge exercise
from an unfamiliar dataset, then explain each column and wait condition in plain
language before coding.
